## The rule

One common approach is to build an emergency fund before investing beyond any employer match: cash set aside for an expense nobody planned, kept where it can be reached within a day or two, and measured in months of essential spending rather than in dollars. The Federal Reserve's survey of household finances uses three months of expenses as its yardstick for a rainy-day fund[^fed-shed-2025]. A larger cushion is worth considering when income is irregular, when one income supports the household, or when finding work in the holder's field tends to take a long time.

PersonalFi measures the cushion as cash on hand divided by a monthly spending figure, and its emergency-fund finding reports how many months of cover that represents.

## Why

Without a cushion, a surprise bill tends to land on a credit card, and a balance carried at card rates costs more every month it stays. The CFPB notes that even a small amount gives some security, and that the right size depends on the household's own history of unexpected costs[^cfpb-emergency-fund]. Measuring in months of essential spending, not total spending, ties the target to what still has to be paid if income stops, and lets it move when the household's costs move.

Where the fund sits matters more than what it earns. The CFPB describes a bank or credit union account as one of the safest places for it[^cfpb-emergency-fund], and deposits at an FDIC-insured bank are insured to {{param:assumptions.market_reference.fdic_insurance_limit}} per depositor, per bank, per ownership category[^fdic-deposit-insurance]. The point of the fund is that it is there, at full value, on the day it is needed; a higher yield that puts that at risk is a poor trade.

## When it does not apply

- **High-interest debt is already in place.** Building the fund and paying the debt down compete for the same dollars; the debt-order page covers the trade-off. One common approach is a small starter cushion first, then the debt, then the full fund.
- **The cash is already spoken for.** Money set aside for a tax payment, a known bill or a sinking fund is not an emergency fund, even when it sits in the same account.
- **Other liquid resources exist.** A household prepared to draw on a taxable investment account may hold less cash, accepting that those assets can be worth less exactly when they are needed.
- **In retirement.** A household drawing on a portfolio usually thinks in terms of a cash reserve for planned withdrawals; the guardrail-withdrawal page covers that.
- **Above the insurance limit.** Cash beyond the limit at one bank, in one ownership category, is not fully covered[^fdic-deposit-insurance].
