## The rule

When an asset class drifts away from its target weight, one common approach is to point new money at whatever is below its target before considering any trade: the next contributions, and dividends and interest where they can be directed rather than reinvested in place[^finra-asset-allocation]. Only the drift that new money cannot close in a reasonable time is a case for trades, and then trades inside tax-advantaged accounts come before trades in a taxable account.

PersonalFi's drift finding names the classes that are off target and by how much, which is what tells new money where to go.

## Why

Redirecting contributions moves the mix back toward its target without realizing a gain and without trading costs. FINRA describes the same idea: adding new money to the lagging asset classes, and notes that rebalancing by selling in a taxable account can mean paying capital gains tax[^finra-asset-allocation]. A gain on a holding kept a year or less is taxed as ordinary income, and a gain on one kept longer at the lower long-term rates[^irs-topic-409]; either way the tax is a cost of the trade.

Inside a retirement account a trade has no tax consequence, which is why trades there come first when trades are needed at all. What matters is the household's mix across every account, not the mix inside each one, so a gap in one account can be closed in another.

## When it does not apply

- **The drift is large.** After a large market move, contributions may take years to close the gap; the household may prefer to accept a trade and its stated cost rather than carry the extra risk that long.
- **No new money.** In retirement, withdrawals do the same job in reverse: taking them from the class that is over its target.
- **The target itself changed.** A planned shift in the mix, such as a glidepath toward bonds, is a plan change rather than drift, and is treated as one.
- **Small drift.** Moves within the app's tolerance band are not findings; chasing every small deviation adds activity without a measurable benefit.
- **A concentrated position.** A single stock or employer stock that dominates the portfolio raises questions beyond rebalancing, which this page does not cover.
